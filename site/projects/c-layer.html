C-Layer

An AI-driven, autonomous multi-layer robotic system for capturing CO2 at the stacks of heavy industry, without stopping the plant.

The problem: risk, not chemistry

Heavy industry produces about a quarter of global CO2 emissions, yet most plants hold back on carbon capture. The chemistry is known. The barrier is operational risk.

  • Downtime is expensive. One hour of unplanned downtime in a cement kiln can cost upwards of $50,000 in cooling and potential damage.
  • The ΔP crisis. When a filter fails, backpressure (ΔP) builds in the stack and can trigger catastrophic equipment failure.
  • Static systems. Conventional capture units are energy-hungry and need manual maintenance, so every filter change interrupts the plant.

How C-Layer compares

The innovation is in the modular integration and autonomous operation, not in a new standalone chemistry.

The solution

An autonomous, modular filtration skid mounted on the exhaust stack, built for continuous capture around the clock.

1

Pre-filtration

Removes dust and large particles from the hot flue gas.

2

MEA absorption

Monoethanolamine grafted onto multi-walled carbon nanotubes binds CO2.

3

CNT absorption

A second carbon-nanotube stage captures the remaining CO2.

4

Structural support

Holds the assembly against airflow pressure and heat.

When a layer saturates, NEMA 34 stepper motors and a push-pull arm on stainless steel guide rails swap it in about 45 seconds. The system is designed to run 24/7 with zero backpressure risk.

Chemistry: CNT and MEA

C-Layer uses a solid-supported amine approach, putting a proven capture chemistry on a high-performance scaffold.

  1. Binding. Monoethanolamine (MEA) molecules are grafted onto multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs).
  2. Efficient regeneration. The solid support has a low specific heat capacity, so releasing the CO2 needs only 1.4-2.5 MJ per kg, by design.
  3. Waste heat recovery. Saturated filters move to an on-site chamber where the factory’s own waste heat releases the CO2, and the cleaned filter goes back into rotation.

Surviving an 870°C stack

Reflective shields

Radiated heat follows the Stefan-Boltzmann law, P = σAT4, so it rises steeply with temperature. The shields are designed to reflect 95% of it.

Vortex tube cooling

Compressed air creates a positive-pressure cold zone that keeps the electronics safe.

Aerogel insulation

Stops heat from conducting into sensitive components.

Fail-safe damper

A spring-return bypass damper, with a backup battery, opens instantly on power failure to protect the furnace.

AI and connectivity

The AI is phased so the system stays reliable before it has data to learn from.

Phase 1

Statistical degradation models

The NVIDIA Jetson Nano fuses CO2, pressure and temperature readings to track filter wear.

Phase 2

LSTM remaining useful life

Once baseline operating data exists, LSTM networks predict when each filter will saturate.

Modbus TCP connects the unit to the plant’s PLCs. MQTT sends data to the cloud for carbon credit verification.

Status and budget

C-Layer is at TRL 2: the concept is defined and the initial blueprints are drawn. A physical prototype has not been built yet because it needs funding.

  • Lab prototype budget: $4,000 to $6,300 for a bench-top proof of concept.
  • Industrial pilot: would need more capital for industrial-grade sensors and specialized thermal shielding.
